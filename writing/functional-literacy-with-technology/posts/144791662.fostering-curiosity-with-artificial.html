From the moment they can speak, children bombard us with questions about anything they can think of, learning all they can about the world. This innate curiosity is a potential tool for learning, yet it diminishes over time due to social pressures, educational systems focused on rote learning, and the overwhelming demands of adult life. In its current state, artificial intelligence systems can change that by rekindling the fire of curiosity and keeping it lit, strenghtening cognitive processes that develop critical thinking and background knowledge, essential for personal and professional growth.

The lasting effects of curiosity

What are atoms made of? Why do I have to learn math? Why won’t printing more money erradicate poverty? These are some of the questions people can ask AI, and it will provide thorough answers. From there, new questions may arise, leading to an iterative process of learning, either by following a chain of previous ideas or through serendipity.

As people continually learn, they can tap into previously acquired knowledge to fill in gaps and make sense of new content. The knowledge, called background knowledge, acts as a bridge to assimilate new content and helps us learn better. [1] We are always building background knowledge, and sometimes it can be transposed to completely unfamiliar situations. Consider a creativity test in which participants are asked to come up with all possible uses for a simple object, like an egg, an umbrella or a toothbrush. In a few minutes, people imagine all sorts of things, relying on their knowledge to reimagine the object, assigning new roles and functions.

The ability to tap into a pool of knowledge when learning leads to several benefits, starting with academics. Students grasp and retain more knowledge if they have been previously exposed to either related content or to content that can be transposed into what they’re learning. Beyond schools, people can rely on their knowledge and experience to solve problems or explore new ideas. By feeding curiosity and building background knowlege, people develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills.

Developing these skills start with fostering curiosity, something that can be enhanced with the aid of AI, as long as there are guardrails in place.

Addressing the AI elephant in the room

Artificial intelligence powered digital products long before the debut of ChatGPT in November 2022. From social media feeds to games and streaming services, algorithms have been searching, sorting, ordering and classifying information based on data. When used for commercial purposes, they are primarily designed to personalize the way people interact with digital products. Learning platforms such as Duolingo and Khan Academy keep students engaged by adjusting the level of dificulty based on how well they are performing, and providing lessons that match their current level of proeficiency. Similarly, companies such as Netflix, Spotify and Meta use AI in their recommendation systems to help people discover content that they may be interested in. These AIs are designed to maximize engagement through personalization, and although they work remarkably well, they have their downsides.

Artificial intelligence systems that predate technologies such as ChatGPT became widely recognized during the 2010s as more digital products were shipped with AI capabilities, in part due to ethical concerns. In 2011, researcher Eli Pariser published “The Filter Bubble”, a book that warns about the dangers posed by personalized algorithmns. [2] Pariser coined the term “filter bubble” to define the uniqueness of each person’s experience when consuming information. He predicted that excessive personalization would isolate communities and erode a common baseline of understanding, undermining democracy. Around the same time, Cambridge Analytica was collecting data from millions of users and their friends on Facebook to build psychological profiles. This data was used to fuel presidential campaigns in 2016. The data misuse became public in 2018 after being disclosed by a former Cambridge Analytica employee. [3]

ChatGPT and similar technologies (called Large Language Models, LLMs) have been created using a massive amount of data, collected from the internet and printed books. Some of the data used to power the AI is possibly copyrighted, and it is not yet clear if companies should be charged with copyright violations. Some countries explicitly forbade using copyrighted data without permission from the authors, while others opted to allow it without prejudice for AI developers. [4] In the meanwhile, artists, writers, designers and other creative professionals have to worry about their work being used without permission to fine tune AI systems.

Beyond data collection and misuse, there’s the issue of the motivation behind some of these tools. Like tradicional AI systems employed for commercial purposes, LLMs are trained to deliver information relevant to users, rather than being correct or factual. Unlike other types of AIs, though, LLMs generate content based on their training data instead of merely reorganizing existing content. When the system is unable to provide an answer based on facts (more precisely, what is stored in its database), it creates one. Since LLMs can craft convincing texts, users may believe the AI-generated content as fact when it may not be. This can have harmful repercussions such as increase in misinformation.

Additionally, the training data or even the algorithm can hold biases that can cause prejudice against social groups, further reinforcing misconceptions and social inequalities.

Despite these limitations, AI can still be used as a tool to foster curiosity and create a lifelong culture of learning. Throughout the 2010s, AI systems not only became more capable of performing a variety of tasks, like helping people learn, but they became safer to use. Governments and organizations worldwide have been creating safeguards to protect people, forcing companies to disclose how they collect and process user data and giving people ownership of their data. Several laws and data privacy initiatives have been created as a result, including European law GDPR (General Data Protection and Regulation) and Brazilian LGPD (General Law of Data Protection). As AI systems improve, more safety mechanisms will be enforced to ensure that we can get the most out of AI with the least exposition to its side effects.

Supercharging curiosity with AI

As a tool, AI can be used in a variety of ways, some of which are good and some of which are not. This is evident in the realm of education. Schools around the world banned ChatGPT shortly after its launch, due to concerns about cheating in homework or writing assignments. [5] Teachers began using AI writing detectors and devised clever ways to spot AI-generated text in their students’ work. As schools better understood how LLM AIs work, bans were lifted, and faculty began incorporating AI into the classroom. Currently, there is no universal consensus on how and to what extent AI should be used in classrooms. Similar to calculators and smartphones in previous decades, AI divides educators into promoters and detractors. The former group supports the use of technology in the classroom and devises ways to blend technology and pedagogy through their lessons. The latter fears that students will become too reliant on tools and may not develop cognitive skills essential for lifelong learning. Both groups present valid points along with evidence. Khan Academy published a research paper stating that students who practice math on their website for 30 minutes a week perform significantly better on math tests than students who do not. [6] On the other hand, standardized tests around the world have been consistently showing stalled or declining grades over the last few years not only on math but also on reading. [7] There is not a single and universally accepted answer that explains good or bad student outcomes as a function of technology, only theories and research in controlled environments. Technology, and AI in particular, is not a universal enhancer of learning but it can assist learners by helping them focus on the tasks that improve learning.

To maximize the benefits of using AI, people should find the middle ground between an AI that doesn’t improve learning and one that solves the problem entirely. This approach is suggested by English professors Suguru Ishizaki and David Kaufer from Carnegie Mellon University. They have been working on software to assist students with writing assignments for decades, believing that technology can soften the cognitive load associated with tasks that do not result in more learning, such as trying to craft the perfect sentence to capture ideas early on during the writing process. [8] They created a tool using Open AI’s ChatGPT where students jot down their ideas in bullet points, and the software expands their ideas into sentences. These sentences serve as steps for developing initial drafts, from which students elaborate further. Their approach, restrained generative AI, pairs the power of AI with several guardrails that prevent the technology to work too much on the task or relying on data from its training data outside the scope of what students are writing about.

Using AI as proposed by Ishizaki and Kaufer keeps learners engaged in the creative process, and doesn’t rob them from the natural struggle that happens when acquiring new knowledge. This struggle is beneficial, as research suggests that it is when we’re transfering knowledge from our working memories to permanent regions in our brain [9]. This approach is not limited to writing assignments. Khanmigo, Khan Academy’s AI chatbot, is another example of a restrained generative AI. Khanmigo helps students learn all the disciplines taught in Khan Academy’s website by acting as a personal tutor. [10] Instead of bluntly giving students the answers for the problems they are working on, Khanmigo focus on further elaborating the problem. It helps students to analyze the problem in which they are stuck from different angles, helping them come up with answers of their own and then evaluating if the answer is right. Khanmigo iterates on this process until students are able to get the correct answers. The AI is capable of pinpointing where each student is struggling and guide them accordingly.

AI can also be used solely for learning, without accompanying tasks. I spent weeks reading out loud for ChatGPT chapters (or cantos) from the English translation of The Divine Comedy, written by Dante Alighieri in the XIV century. The AI helped me understand each canto and provided answers for all my questions. Some of these questions sparked new ones unrelated to Alighieri’s work, such as questions about the historical, political, economical and geographical perspectives of Middle Ages’ Italy. I also learned about the Greek and Christian influences that are present in The Divine Comedy, how the poems are organized and how Alighieri’s work is tied to the unification of Italy at the dawn of the Renaissance period. By providing timely and precise information, the AI helped me focus on learning and kept me curious to know more about the world depicted in The Divine Comedy, and the world in which Dante Alighieri lived.

Ask more questions

In its current state, AI can bring about fundamental changes in how education happens. Over the last few decades, technology allowed learning to go remote, self-paced and personalized. Despite these advancements, education itself remained constant. Whether asyncronous or not, the building blocks of learning — content — were static. Textbooks were digitized, lesson plans were put online, and technology primarily served as a medium for education. The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), introduced in late 2022, has shown that these blocks can be malleable. Content is now adjustable to each learner’s needs, something previously achievable only by a personal tutor.

AI is not the definitive solution to all learning problems. Current AIs are riddled with biases, they can produce convincingly false information, and require massive amounts of energy and computing power to operate. Moreover, AI can be misused as a learning tool, potentially taking away the opportunity for students to struggle with problems and internalize new knowledge. In the long run, AI can prevent students from developing essential skills for academic success, personal development, and the workplace.

To ensure AI is safe, governments and organizations worldwide are creating safeguards to limit its destructive potential while maximizing its benefits.

Learners have a powerful tool to shape their learning journey at their fingertips, and all it takes to make that tool work is a question. Like logs that fuel a campfire, questions fed into the AI result in answers that satisfy and expand the burning curiosity. Somewhere along the lives of many, the fire dims and eventually goes out, and many questions are eventually forgotten, like unused chopped logs stored in a dark room. AI provides the necessary tools to rekindle the fire of curiosity; all it needs is some fuel. All of us have that fuel inside our heads, even if it isn’t apparent at first. To test this, open an AI chatbot, start a conversation and throw in the first question or task. From there, let the exchange between you and the AI happen and see where it goes. Ask more questions, engage with the answers and satisfy your inner curiosity, keeping alive the desire to learn and transforming curiosity into a lifelong habit.
