With some exceptions, global learning rates have dropped significantly after the COVID-19 pandemic, as evidenced by students’ poor performance on standardized tests such as the National Assessment of Basic Education (SAEB) in Brazil or the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). Discussions about this data suggested that the lack of preparedness of schools and families to adopt a remote lifestyle, inequalities associated with access or mastery of technology, and the increase of mental health problems due to isolation are the main culprits. Another line of thought to consider is that the instruction delivered with the aid of technology may not have achieved the same results as traditional classes.

As education went remote, and then hybrid with the aid of technology, pedagogy didn’t follow. Teachers use the same methods, curriculum, and rubrics to teach and evaluate but delivered over video-conferencing software. Factoring in the quality of instruction delivered through technology can explain the role and importance of technological tools in education. [1]

Learning and environment

How does learning happen? I heard from a former K-12 reading instruction teacher that you need just one thing to foster the habit of reading books: for books, readers, and reading to find one another. Without books, there are readers but no reading. Without readers, there is no reading. Finally, without reading, books and readers don’t find one another. The contents of books become meaningful once reading happens. Likewise, I believe that learning happens when teachers [2], students, and content work together under the guidance of meaningful instruction.

What does learning, under this definition, look like in different environments, such as in-person classrooms, asynchronous MOOCs (Massive Online Open Courses), or video-conferencing? With exceptions, instruction is the same across all environments, hampering student learning.

Instruction is essential for learning. As research [3] suggests, instruction design can profoundly impact learning. One design choice that can influence learning outcomes is the choice of the environment. There are many ways to deliver instruction, such as in-person classes, radio, television, and e-learning apps, to cite a few.

Instruction delivered through digital technologies doesn't necessarily improve student learning or increase teacher productivity. If the environment changes without proper adjustments to instruction, the effect is an increased strain on working memory, reducing the ability to retain knowledge. [4] One example is analyzing the differences between reading a printed and a digital book. While printed books usually have a consistent physical and informational architecture, digital books are read on different devices, many of which aren’t designed solely for reading. Furthermore, finding and retrieving information from digital books is more complex than skimming through the pages of a printed book. [5]

E-learning has similar challenges. Applying the same instructional practices from in-person classes to technology-enabled ones doesn't deliver the same results, since learners must, in addition to understanding the content, master the technology required to participate in the class.

Edtechs and schools should consider appropriate distinctions between environment, content, and instruction when designing effective instruction for learners and teachers.

Edtechs and effective instruction delivery

Edtechs can completely transform classroom experiences, bringing assumptions and implications that can ultimately affect learning. How to best measure their effectiveness and ensure that technology maximizes student learning?

In traditional learning environments, teachers act as the sole mediums of instruction. Teaching is done by providing students with content and practice tasks to test their newly acquired knowledge. Finally, schools administer exams to evaluate whether students have learned or not. School faculty then combine this data with qualitative data collected from classroom instruction to devise strategies to improve learning [6].

Meanwhile, technology-enabled learning environments bring fundamental changes to the classroom, requiring different approaches to measure and improve learning.

First, students aren't passively absorbing knowledge. They are protagonists of their learning and, as such, are expected to have autonomy by devising learning strategies best suited to their lifestyles and needs. [3]

Second, instead of exposing learners to content, teachers become guides, helping students search, navigate, and assimilate relevant information.

Third, technology becomes instrumental for learning to happen, as opposed to being merely assistive. It is the infrastructure on top of which teachers and students collaborate.

Given the importance of technology for learning, there should be a mechanism to isolate and measure the effectiveness of instruction delivered through technology.

One way this is done is by analyzing student engagement with edtech products. Independent research studies suggest that students who use these products more often have better learning outcomes. [7] There is no evidence, though, that engagement causes improvement. Students may be more engaged with coursework in general, meaning they would have fared better with any tool, including paper and pen. At best, current research describes a correlation, not a relationship of cause and effect.

Engagement alone doesn’t fully explain student learning. Another aspect to consider is the pedagogical assumptions embedded in the product. School teachers and administrators should question edtechs about the pedagogical structure of their programs, to understand if the company is providing products that are:

  1. Based on learning research backed by evidence of effectiveness for a sample of students that is representative of the school or district;

  2. Aligned with the values and beliefs of both schools and families, as well as the curriculum adopted by the school.

Just as with improving instruction by collecting data from grades and classroom observations, schools must evaluate the effectiveness of edtechs using both measures of engagement and pedagogical approaches.

Closing thoughts

The edtech market gained traction in recent years [8]. All over the world, companies created and expanded products to support every aspect of teaching and learning. This contrasts with low student performances on national and international standardized assessment tests. Given the unique physical and psychological challenges presented during and after the public health crisis and the reinvention of the classroom overnight, we may never know with exact precision how much of this performance can be explained by the structure of edtech programs alone, much less make them accountable for student performance during the pandemic. We’re not living in a global pandemic at the time of this writing, but the use of technology to mediate instruction is here to stay nonetheless. Edtechs have their understanding of how to design technology to deliver instruction, potentially limiting the role of teachers in the learning process while making students more dependent on technology to learn. Edtechs should be accountable for student learning to ensure that their commercial products align with the values and needs of the learning community.
