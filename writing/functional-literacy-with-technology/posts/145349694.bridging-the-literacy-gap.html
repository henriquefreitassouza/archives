In the 1980s, researchers Lee James and Warren Schirtzinger coined the term chasm [1] to explain the wide views of disruptive technologies among different groups of people. The chasm separates technology enthusiasts, those who are willing to test and improve disruptive technologies, from pragmatists, who demand from technology providers working proof of their products and minimal to no disruption of their current systems. This theoretical divide can, to some extent, be used to make a second classification of people: those who are literate in technology and those who are not. This literacy chasm is widening in a post-COVID-19 pandemic world with artificial intelligence (AI) that can curate and generate information, leaving a growing number of people out of school and the job market. Consulting firms and books provide recipes to help technology companies cross the chasm and win their market segments. Can the same be done to help more people cross the literacy chasm and increase their chances of success in a technology-driven society?

A brief history of the literacy chasm

Technology is evolving at breakneck speed. Companies and governments around the world are racing amongst themselves to develop faster, better, and cheaper technologies to improve human lives. A by-product of each new discovery is a small-scale earthquake, creating knowledge cracks that ultimately give rise to the literacy chasm. This is not something new, but the chasm has been widening fast over the last few decades.

In 1965, Gordon Moore, Intel’s co-founder and former CEO, published a paper called Cramming more components onto integrated circuits [2], predicting that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit would double every two years. The theory, branded Moore’s law and revisited since its original publication, became an industry standard, as vendors kept pushing the limits of technology to create ever more powerful chips. This standard has had wide effects across many industries that assembled electronic devices, leading to the development of better hardware at a fraction of the cost [3]. Powerful and cheap computers left the confinements of university labs and corporate offices to find their way into the homes of millions of people around the world after the 80s. These devices became known as personal computers (PCs). Not long after, PCs were connected with one another and exchanging messages over the internet, an interconnected grid of cables, satellites, and radio waves. As technology evolved, only a handful of people engaged in its construction and direction, leading to different forms of inequalities in technology literacy, such as the literacy gap between men and women [4]. Despite the growing efforts of companies and activist groups to correct these inequalities, the pace with which technology evolves creates new challenges to address them.

At this point, the stage is set for the introduction of a number of disruptive innovations that are shaping societies and institutions around the world, and marginalizing those that are unable to keep up with the changes brought about by technology. During the 80s and 90s, software outstripped hardware as the main source of revenue in the technology industry, led by a handful of companies providing operating systems and desktop applications for industries and individuals alike. In a short span of years, hardware and software integrated into our work and personal lives, demanding from individuals an increasing set of skills to fully participate in society. Throughout the 2000s and 2010s, two major developments catapulted the power of software and led us to the present moment: the rise of smartphones and cloud computing. Being freed from the constraints of fixed places and having access to powerful computing devices off-premise, vendors flooded the market with software as capable as desktop applications and less dependent on any individual device’s hardware capabilities. As a result, a newborn child today leaves her digital footprint on government databases and social media long before she learns how to stand on her legs. Children learn how to scroll through Instagram and TikTok before learning how to read. Adolescents and adults order food, study, work, and socialize online. Less than two years prior to the publication of this article, people began to rely on artificial intelligence to perform tasks that, not long ago, could only be done by human beings. Technology is not a mere tool anymore: it is an indispensable companion to belong and engage in the digital society.

Social and economic consequences of the chasm

The technology literacy gap widened as it became part of everyday life. The number of barriers to literacy increased for those on both sides of the chasm, but with more serious consequences at the illiterate end. Many people around the world can’t vote, study, work, access government services, or shop without access to and proficiency in technology. Meanwhile, the gap is exploited by cybercriminal activity and further division arises from biases in the design and behavior of technology, generating worldwide economic impact.

Accessibility is a roadblock to economic growth and social inclusion. About half of the global population doesn’t have access to the internet [5]. Despite computers and smartphones being more affordable when compared with previous decades, their costs are still prohibitive for many. Almost 9% of the global population lives below the poverty line [6], and minimum wages in many countries aren’t enough to cover more than living costs. Another barrier to accessibility comes in the forms of literacy and numeracy. About 13% of people aged 15 years or more worldwide are illiterate [7]. Knowledge of written words and elementary mathematics correlates with rising living standards and economic growth. As technology automates more mundane and repetitive tasks, workers must master basic literacy skills to work in jobs that require higher levels of creativity and problem-solving. Moreover, increasing digital inclusion by 10 percentage points could generate up to a 1.21% increase in GDP in developed economies and 1.38% in developing ones [8].

Among those able to regularly use technology and access the internet, bias is a major concern. Biases translated into data change the behavior of software, generating output that can be harmful to certain groups, especially those that are already underrepresented and marginalized. Biases can also reinforce values and beliefs held by certain groups, skewing our perception of reality and making us less tolerant of different opinions and views [9]. As AI entrenches itself into our everyday lives with biased data, these systems can deny credit loans and job or university applications based on gender or race [10]. Similar biases are seen in the development of other technological products such as air conditioners [11] and cars [12]. Not only the data processed by software can be biased but technology itself can be. In the United States, employment in technology is biased toward white men [13], who form a majority in the discussions concerning the development of technology. In Brazil, only 15,3% of all students who obtained a bachelor’s degree in computer science in 2022 were women [14]. Leaving minority groups out of the table poses the danger of perpetuating discrimination and misrepresentation. As technology becomes more integral for society, this translates into further marginalization of these groups, reducing their likelihood to study, work, and access government services.

Another challenge for technological societies to overcome is cybercrime. Cybercrime has cost more than USD 8 trillion dollars globally in 2023 [15], more than 7% of global GDP [16] that year. Cybercriminals are tech-savvy individuals who study ways to exploit systems, and their tactics aren’t limited by breaching security measures imposed by these systems. Phishing is the fastest-growing form of attack, representing 53.2% of all cyberattacks of 2022 [17], a year in which an estimated USD 7.1 trillion dollars were lost due to criminal activity. Phishing is a form of scam used by criminals to deceive people into giving them sensitive information. It is a form of attack with many variants, from unsuspecting emails to phone calls and websites that look exactly like their original counterparts. Criminals replicate official channels of communication used by governments and companies, impersonating them to attack victims. Although this form of attack has existed for more than two decades, it still works because systems are a combination of technology and humans [18]. Phishing tactics make use of persuasive psychological tricks and exploit the inability of people to differentiate a real email or website from its fake copies, a problem rooted in the lack of technology literacy.

Paving the way towards literacy

Fortunately, governments, companies, and civic organizations are taking steps to recognize and address the need to make technology more inclusive, equalitarian, and safe from its inception. In 2022, Brazil’s federal government published an addendum to the new national standardized curriculum [19] (BNCC), first released in 2017, to include technology literacy as a mandatory discipline for all public and private schools in the country. The curriculum, spanning the entire K-12 spectrum, is grounded in three pillars: computational thinking, digital world, and digital culture. Students are required to learn topics such as problem-solving, algorithms, software development, and online safety. This curriculum can enable students to critically evaluate the role of technology in shaping society and incentivize them to pursue further studies and careers in the tech industry.

Meanwhile, organizations launch an increasing number of initiatives, from workshops and courses to affirmative job opportunities and scholarships, all geared towards increasing the number of women and other misrepresented or underrepresented groups in STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) programs and jobs within the technology industry. One such initiative is Programmers of Tomorrow [20], an NGO focused on employing underrepresented groups in Brazil’s tech industry. Through a network of volunteers and donors, they provide software development and English classes. Students receive at no cost a computer and broadband internet access at home to study. Some schools allow students to obtain degrees and pay only when they’re employed with a certain minimum wage with the help of financial partners such as Principia [21]. Reducing or exempting costs greatly reduces the entry barrier for people to pursue a career in technology, as even elementary courses and materials can be expensive.

Tackling inequalities not only increases living standards, but fills in some of the many vacant job posts currently open, and some of those that are still to come. Demand for technology professionals will increase by up to 35% until 2027 worldwide [22]. The Brazilian government estimates almost 800.000 jobs in Information Technology (IT) [23] related roles by 2025, but universities across the country aren’t conferring enough degrees to support this growing industry. In 2022, 1.2 million students were enrolled in IT programs, but less than 250.000 have graduated [14]. Companies around the world have been investing in preparing workers to meet this demand by creating internal universities and boot camps to either upskill their current workforce or train new people to fill tech roles. Some multinational organizations such as Mercado Livre [25] and CI&T [26] have developed courses as part of their application process. Candidates enroll in these courses and learn about the industry with current employees. Others, such as Accenture [27], have developed extensive programs to upskill their current workers. Finally, to recognize and address inequalities in the workforce such as the gender and race gap, and the presence of minority groups and people with disabilities, companies like Google [28] have developed policies to hire and train people from diverse backgrounds and characteristics. All of these initiatives ensure that technology is built by and for all people.

In addition to accessibility and equality, investments are being made to ensure online safety. The cybersecurity industry is growing by 12.4% annually [29]. In April 2024, Brazilian bank Nubank launched a feature for its banking app called verified call [30], to help people recognize legitimate phone calls from the bank and prevent scams. While on a phone call with someone claiming to be a representative of the bank, customers can verify the legitimacy of the call by opening the app. This is part of a larger campaign by the company to fight against online fraud, which has been growing in Latin America [31]. Cyberattacks are more common in underdeveloped countries due to having a growing population of internet users who don’t know how to safely use it. Educational programs across the world try to mitigate the issue with curriculums designed for learners of different ages. In 2017, Google launched Be Internet Awesome [32], a program designed to teach kids safe habits and behavior when browsing the internet. The curriculum, which can be used in classrooms, is divided into five segments, covering topics such as personal data management, sharing content on social media, and avoiding misinformation and disinformation. To fully reap the benefits of digital citizenship, people must be aware of the dangers of being online and how to avoid them, the same way they know how to avoid dangerous places.

Technology literacy is a building block for digital citizenship

Technology is evolving fast, and the pace of changes is accelerating. It is harder today to understand and assimilate these changes than just a few decades ago. Whether we know it or not, our data travels all around the world through a global network of machines. This data is in the hands of governments, companies, family members, and even criminals. Even unknowingly, we produce tiny bits of data by simply interacting with machines. When we log in to our social network accounts and hit the like button on pictures of cats, a political stance, or the picture of the newborn child of a dear friend, we’re signaling companies about our preferences, feeding computer programs that are designed to know our tastes and show us more of what we like with the intent of influencing our behavior. Similarly, by searching for information online, we’re sharing our needs with search engines, and by extension, their advertisers. We don’t even need to actively engage with a machine to have our data leaking into the internet. Digital governments around the world centralize their institutions and services in a single place, which includes their citizens’ data. In a sense, we already are digital citizens, but many people don’t realize that. Half of the global population is unplugged from the digital society, unable to participate in it in any form whatsoever. Even some of those who are online can’t properly exert their digital citizenship, as they’re being denied credit loans or job offerings due to the unquestionable and cryptic black box of computer-made decisions. Although computer programs are able to analyze data faster and with more precision than humans, they may be imbued with the same biases as their human creators, perpetuating and even increasing long-standing inequalities that marginalize people and shrink the economy as a whole. Bad actors are keenly aware of these biases and other security vulnerabilities in the systems that form the backbone of our digital society, exploiting people for their own benefit and generating trillions of dollars of economic damage every year.

We are far from solving all problems that keep marginalized groups in the non-literacy side of the chasm, but many organizations and individuals are engaged in changing that, starting with education. Children young enough to attend kindergarten are being taught about computational thinking and the digital world in schools, with curriculums that span the whole K-12 education. With proper guidance from an early age, students will be able to understand the role of technology in shaping our society and pursue careers in technology and STEM, balancing out the unequal distribution of jobs in the technology sector to include more members of underrepresented groups. Scholarships and study loans help thousands of people across the world to pursue higher education, while college and corporate policies ensure that minority groups can attend classes and obtain industry-recognized degrees. Furthermore, a handful of companies are actively engaged in training the workforce to occupy jobs that require proficiency in technology and creativity skills. Many job openings are exclusive or give preference to minority groups, ensuring that a more diverse group of people enter the workforce. Through collective effort, we’re shrinking the literacy gap and ensuring that technology serves as a strong foundation, not a barrier, for a thriving digital society.
